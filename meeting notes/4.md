Meeting Summary — AI Governance Modernization: Strategy, Policy, Model Inventory & Enterprise Integration

Meeting focus: Align on AI Governance priorities, understand existing governance policies and processes, define the target-state model and agent inventory, explore enterprise-wide intake and integration, and establish how AI Governance and Engineering should partner.

Overall takeaway: The team wants to transform AI Governance from a largely manual, reactive process into an automated, policy-driven, lifecycle-based governance platform with centralized inventory, risk-based reviews, deployment readiness controls, auditability, and enterprise-wide visibility.

Note: Portions of the transcript have transcription errors, particularly in the Chinese-English sections. I have captured the clearest discussion points and distinguished current capabilities from future goals.

1. Key Discussion Notes

A. AI Governance Background and Modernization Goals

The AI Governance function was established approximately three years ago, initially with a small team. Dedicated engineering support was established more recently.

Current challenges

* Many governance processes remain manual.
* Model registration depends heavily on owners self-reporting.
* Governance teams spend considerable time collecting information and following up.
* Models may already be in production before complete inventory and compliance information is captured.
* Governance data is distributed across multiple systems.
* Limited engineering capacity has delayed important capabilities, including auditability.

Modernization goal: Automate governance processes, improve data quality and visibility, and proactively enforce requirements throughout the AI lifecycle.

B. Shift from Reactive Reviews to Proactive Governance

A major strategic direction is to move governance activities earlier in the AI lifecycle.

Current state

Model developed / deployed

Owner self-reports

Governance collects missing information

Manual compliance follow-up

Target state

Use case registered early

Risk assessed and controls identified

Compliance requirements verified

Deployment readiness signal

The desired future state includes integration with deployment environments, enabling platform owners to determine whether a model has satisfied required governance controls before production deployment.

This was discussed as a future goal, not an existing production capability.

C. Integrate AI Use Case Intake and Model Inventory

The current use case intake and model inventory processes are separate, but the goal is to connect them into one lifecycle-based solution.

Lifecycle stage	Governance purpose
Ideation / Evaluation	Register the AI use case and perform initial risk review
Development	Capture relevant model and solution information and required controls
Pre-production	Confirm inventory completeness and deployment readiness
Production	Maintain inventory, ownership, deployment status, and compliance information
Post-deployment	Support ongoing monitoring, reassessment, and lifecycle updates

A key point was that information captured during early use case intake should be reused for model inventory registration.

Expected benefits: Less duplicate data entry, more complete records, better traceability, and continuous governance visibility.

D. AI Governance Policy Drives Platform Requirements

The AI Governance team emphasized that all workflows, reviews, controls, and registration requirements originate from established policies and standards.

Existing documentation reportedly includes:

* AI Governance policies and standards.
* A mapping of policy requirements to model lifecycle stages.
* Required registration information.
* AI use case review criteria and risk considerations.
* Governance decisions and stakeholder communication requirements.

The team offered to share a policy-focused presentation and a broader enterprise Data and AI Governance organizational overview.

Important takeaway: The engineering team should understand the policy framework before defining automation logic, approvals, data requirements, or system controls.

E. Enterprise Universal AI Intake Initiative

A broader initiative is underway to unify AI service intake across different CVS organizations and technology teams.

The discussion covered several AI categories, including:

* Generative AI solutions and agents.
* Traditional machine learning and predictive models.
* Data science use cases.
* AI applications developed or provisioned through different platforms.

The desired approach is to reduce fragmented intake processes and coordinate governance, architecture, provisioning, and other reviews.

However, the transcript does not clearly establish whether all reviews will be sequential, parallel, or triggered conditionally.

Opportunity: The new AI Governance platform should align with universal intake rather than create another disconnected registration experience.

F. Risk-Based AI Governance Reviews

The governance process should distinguish between lower-risk and higher-risk AI use cases.

Example	Governance implications
Lower-risk internal AI use case, without sensitive patient data	Potentially streamlined governance review, subject to applicable policies
AI solution handling sensitive information	Additional privacy, security, or data protection review may be required
Patient-facing or clinically significant AI	Stronger governance, clinical/patient-safety, privacy, and other relevant reviews may be necessary

The discussion emphasized that AI Governance does not independently own every review.

Other teams may own:

* Enterprise Architecture and cloud provisioning reviews.
* Cybersecurity and security assessments.
* Privacy and protected health information controls.
* Clinical and patient-safety evaluations.

Goal: Establish clear handoffs, avoid duplicate reviews, and apply the appropriate controls based on actual AI risk.

G. Centralized AI and Agent Inventory

A longer-term priority is establishing a comprehensive repository of AI models, agents, use cases, and governance information.

Potential information to capture includes:

Data category	Examples
AI asset identification	Use case ID, model ID, agent information
Ownership	Business owner, technical owner, responsible team
Lifecycle	Development, deployment, production status
Risk and compliance	Risk classification, controls, review outcomes
Architecture	Platform, integration, hosting/deployment information
Monitoring	Model behavior, agent performance, observability metrics
Auditability	Governance decision history, actions, and supporting evidence

The platform could eventually serve as an enterprise information source for Governance, Security, Privacy, Architecture, and other stakeholders.

A related idea was a centralized governance data hub, although its architecture and ownership have not yet been finalized.

H. Observability, Auditability, and Compliance Evidence

A specific concern raised by Engineering was the need to capture and audit governance decisions and relevant reasoning steps.

The Governance team agreed this is important but acknowledged that limited capacity has constrained prioritization.

Future requirements may include:

* Traceable governance decisions and associated rationale.
* Records of reviewer approvals and control assessments.
* Historical changes to AI assets.
* Deployment status and lifecycle events.
* Agent behavior and performance metrics.
* Data sharing with cybersecurity and observability teams.

Key distinction: Registration and inventory modernization are the immediate foundation. Comprehensive auditability and observability are important capabilities that still need prioritization and technical design.

I. Partnership, Expectations, and Communication

Both sides agreed that a strong working relationship between Governance and Engineering is essential.

Discussion themes included:

* Engineering should first learn the current policies, processes, and organizational responsibilities.
* Governance can provide documentation, background, and stakeholder introductions.
* Engineering should contribute both strategic thinking and practical execution.
* Priorities should reflect governance needs and engineering feasibility.
* Regular communication, potentially including weekly one-on-one meetings, would help maintain alignment.
* Leadership communication should connect modernization work with measurable business outcomes.

6. Key Takeaways for Leadership

1. Modernize the inventory first. The largest immediate governance gap is reliance on manual model-owner reporting, which can leave production assets incompletely registered.

2. Connect intake with inventory. Reusing AI use case registration data throughout the model lifecycle can reduce manual work and support more complete governance records.

3. Shift governance earlier in delivery. The desired future state is to verify required governance controls before deployment instead of discovering issues after production release.

4. Integrate with enterprise functions. AI Governance needs to work alongside enterprise intake, architecture, security, privacy, and patient safety without duplicating their responsibilities.

5. Build auditability and observability into the roadmap. Decision traceability, lifecycle status, and AI agent monitoring will become increasingly valuable as the platform scales.

6. Strengthen the Governance–Engineering partnership. Clear priorities, shared understanding of policies, regular communication, and proactive technical recommendations will help move the program from manual operations toward enterprise automation.

7. Recommended Immediate Priorities

From an AI Governance Engineering leadership perspective, the most useful next steps are to obtain the policy and organizational documents, develop a current-state process map, and align with the Governance team on the target-state lifecycle.

That creates the foundation for three tangible deliverables:

* Governance workflow map: Connect intake, risk assessment, approval, inventory, deployment, and monitoring requirements.
* Integration and gap assessment: Identify where Quickbase, the new governance platform, universal intake, and deployment systems should exchange information.
* Prioritized modernization roadmap: Separate foundational commitments from longer-term auditability, monitoring, and automated compliance capabilities.

Overall recommendation: Treat the modernization as an end-to-end AI lifecycle governance program, not simply a registry replacement. The long-term value will come from combining policy-driven workflows, authoritative inventory, deployment readiness, and enterprise-wide risk visibility.
